A token backed by rooftop units.

Nearly every token is backed by the expectation that somebody will pay more for it later. This one is backed by service calls, changeouts and maintenance contracts — a licensed mechanical shop in Southfield that was billing customers for twenty years before it wrote a line of Solidity.

What moves the number

MTYLD is an ERC-4626 vault holding USDC. Its share value is simply the assets in it divided by the shares outstanding, and exactly one thing moves that ratio.

Effect on value per share

Someone deposits USDCno change
Someone redeems their sharesno change
A hundred people pile in at onceno change
Mechanical Temp reports revenue↑ rises for everyone

Deposits and redemptions move assets and shares together, so the ratio holds. Reporting revenue adds assets and mints no new shares — which is why it lifts the value for every holder at once and can't dilute anyone.

Two things it is not

It isn't a working-capital facility

Deposits stay in the vault. The contract has no function that moves them to the business, to the operator, or anywhere else — you can read it yourself. Money in the vault is not money the shop is spending on equipment.

It isn't a yield product

There's no rate, no APY, and nothing accrues on a schedule. If the shop has a slow month, the number sits exactly where it is. Nothing is emitted, nothing is farmed, and no treasury prints tokens to make a percentage look good.

The locked shares, and why they exist

Share value is assets ÷ shares. If the share count is ever allowed to approach zero while assets remain — one full withdrawal leaving dust behind — that division produces nonsense. This already happened once, on a sibling vault, where the price ran to over a million dollars a share and the funds had to be pulled out.

So this vault ships with three guards. Virtual shares put a floor under the divisor mathematically. A dead-share position was minted at launch to a burn address, unredeemable by anyone including the operator, so real supply never returns to zero. And a withdrawal floor is asserted on every redemption in case someone refactors the first two away.

The gauge on the vault page shows what portion of supply is that locked position. It's large now because the vault is small; it shrinks toward nothing as deposits grow.

Where the fee goes

DepositingNothing. No entry fee — you're not taxed for showing up.
HoldingNothing. No management fee, no drag on the share value.
Redeeming1% of what you withdraw, paid to Mechanical Temp.

That 1% is the shop's cut, and it's capped at 3% by a constant compiled into the contract — a ceiling nobody can raise, including the operator. Fees leave the vault immediately rather than sitting inside it pretending to be depositor assets, which would corrupt the share value.

What could go wrong

Revenue reporting is voluntary. The contract cannot compel Mechanical Temp to report anything. If the shop stops, the number stops. That is a trust relationship with a business, not a guarantee enforced by code, and no amount of Solidity changes it.

The exit fee means short round trips lose money. Deposit and redeem the same day and you're down 1% for nothing. This only makes sense held across reporting periods.

The operator can pause deposits and redemptions. Existing positions aren't touched by a pause, but you can't exit during one.

Reporting into a nearly-empty vault mostly benefits the locked shares. Early on, a large share of any reported revenue lands on a position nobody can redeem. It's an argument for reporting after deposits arrive, not before.

Unaudited, on a testnet. The NAV math is verified against a reproducible simulation covering full exits, donation attacks and redeposits with zero value leak. That's not a professional review, and every dollar here is play money.
